Project 00 · Camera & Perspective

One subject.
Many perspectives.

Three experiments reveal a simple photographic truth: perspective comes from where the camera stands. Zoom changes the framing; distance changes the geometry.

The wrong way vs. the right way

Selfies & portrait perspective

I photographed Anton and myself at four zoom levels, stepping farther away each time so our faces remained roughly the same size in the frame.

Why does the second portrait look better?

Distance—not zoom—changes the face.

At very close range, the nose is proportionally much closer to the camera than the ears, so it projects larger while the sides of the face fall away. Stepping back makes those depth differences small compared with the total camera distance, producing flatter, more natural-looking proportions. Zooming in only restores the original framing after the camera moves back.

The focal-length labels describe 35 mm-equivalent angle of view. Apple lists 13 mm at 0.5× and 26 mm at 1×; the 5× and 10× values are approximate 26 mm-based digital-crop equivalents. The softness at high zoom is a resolution trade-off, separate from the perspective change. Camera specifications ↗

02

The same principle in reverse

Architectural perspective compression

I first photographed the facade from across the field with zoom, then walked toward the building and photographed it without zoom while keeping the building at a similar scale.

What creates the compression?

Camera position controls relative scale.

From far away, the distance from the camera to the near and far parts of the building differs only slightly, so they project at nearly the same relative size. Up close, that distance ratio grows: nearby surfaces loom while farther surfaces shrink. The long view feels compressed because zoom crops a narrow field of view from a distant position—it does not flatten space by itself.

03

The Vertigo shot

The dolly zoom

I moved the camera backward while increasing zoom, trying to hold Totoro at roughly the same size as the room changed around it.

Animated result

Totoro stays. The room moves.

Because camera distance and focal length change together, the subject stays near the center at a similar scale while the background’s perspective transforms. The wide, close frame stretches the table and reveals more of the room; the distant, zoomed frame flattens those depth cues and narrows the field of view.

  1. 01Lock the subject near the center.
  2. 02Step backward between each still.
  3. 03Increase zoom to recover the subject’s size.

The animation reverses after the 15× frame so the spatial change is easy to inspect in both directions. This is the still-photo version of the classic dolly zoom ↗.
